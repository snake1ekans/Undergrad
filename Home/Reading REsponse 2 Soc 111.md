

I've spent a lot of time thinking about how to go about discussing the podcast "How Women Became America's Safety Net." As a (hopefully) future medical practitioner, I understand the role that that I will play in that safety net. Understanding how America's culture and society shapes the way we view women is a crucial part of being a doctor. When I started listening to the podcast, I started to think about the ways in which I see society failing women today. I think it's quite easy to lose our sociological imagination and broad perspective and only focus on individual circumstances. However, a greater understanding of the whole system is essential to understand the ways in which we can restructure and improve society for women and especially caregiving women. 

In order to understand where we are today, I think it's essential to turn back the clock. The podcast discussed the history of women in the workplace, especially in World War II, but I think we can examine even further back in order to understand how we got to where we are today. The root of modern inequality for women comes from the societal restructuring of the industrial revolution and the ways in which it changed family and the workforce. What initially started as an unequal but not concrete social dynamic of women's labor being generally focused in homemaking and men's labor focused on production shifted as the burden of labor became a more unifying effort. 

Subsistence farming and serfdom gave way to industrialization, which shifted the focus of labor to essentially make binary what was a bimodal trend. While many, self included, have their critiques of Karl Marx's understanding of industrial society, it is hard to argue that his understanding of the division of labor between laborers and owners was not a prescient one. Furthermore, other, more aspirational and well-thought-out writers have argued that Marx's analysis was limited to direct exchange of goods for labor, and entirely ignored the essential role that women play in maintaining society.

This brings us to discuss the conversation in the podcast around Roosevelt's democratic socialist policies as laid out in the New Deal, and the ways in which World War II affected how labor was structured in the United States. Notable in its exclusions, the new deal carved out exemptions for much of its social programs specifically for agriculture workers, a predominantly Black-dominated industry, and domestic laborers, which was predominated by women and especially Black women. This is something I wish had been mentioned a little bit more in the podcast: the structural barriers erected by men that stood in the way of women's compensation for their contribution.

The destruction of New Deal and Great Society policies, driven in large part by racism and the conservative and neoliberal politics of 80s Republicans and Bill Clinton, brought an end to any nominal compensation programs for low-class working families and in doing so exposed a trap that was laid out in the first case study of the podcast: a mother, made suddenly single through no fault of her own, was trapped in an impossible Catch-22: she could work less, spend time with her kids, and apply for a slim chance at meager welfare benefits, or she could work 3 jobs, have a higher chance at even less benefits, and spend her weekdays with her kids and weekends with fellow laborers in the factory.

This case study lays bare a larger issue in society: America's Social structure is thoroughly and structurally anti-family. Even the "perfect" nuclear family as it was presented in 1950s propaganda, in which the perfect white father returns home to the perfect drone housewife and perfect Ricky and Jane, demonstrates that American society is structured to fracture relationships between people and communities. That same "nuclear family" fails to include the influence of grandparents, neighbors, cousins, and friends, crucial supporters in the labor predominated by women.

This destruction of the family comes at a cost: the systemic undervaluing of labor. While men's labor is consistently undervalued, labor done in the home, unpaid, is mostly or entirely uncompensated by society. We expect our women to be perfect baby-making and baby-raising factories, and if they dare commit the moral failing of marrying an abusive man, or dare to do such a thing as allow their husband to die, then we condemn them to a life of double servitude: the servitude of maintaining a dignified life for herself and those in her care, and the servitude of selling her labor and time for a meager recompense, often further undercut by her "lack of stability" or "lack of professional skills."

Women's labor is truly essential. The foundations of modern leftist movements are based on the ideal that not only is it a fundamentally moral thing to ensure that every human lives in dignity, but that that dignity is maintained just as much societally, by roadworkers, dockhands, and factory laborers, as it is individually by domestic carers, cleaners, and other roles traditionally filled by women. While demographics of who works where has begun to shift, the fact still remains, as noted in the podcast: women are far far more likely to go undercompensated or uncompensated for their labor. If their labor is structurally recognized, it is often in "lower-position" roles, such as secretary or cleaner, and if their labor is unrecognized, then the best they get from men is jokes about how they belong in the kitchen or deeply unfunny comedians commenting on their clothing choices during their only free time.

The underlying point, and one argued fairly effectively if not directly by Dr Calarco, is that women's lack of compensation is an enormous societal ill, and one that is starting to be addressed in very limited ways in small scopes. However, the broader project of ensuring fair compensation is far bigger than single-state pilot programs and think pieces about if "girlboss feminism" was actually good for women or not. The reality is that women's lack of compensation is symptomatic of a much larger societal ill, but one that falls disproportionately on the very people that birth, raise, and sustain our society. Women's liberation and just compensation through the restructuring of society must be our greatest project, but it will only be achieved through a larger restructuring that focuses on repairing the other societal ills. Just as women were a casualty in the New Deal's exclusions that were targeted at Black Americans, we must understand that inequality anywhere is a threat to equality anywhere, and that the milquetoast single state projects and waffling of the podcast's host will get us nowhere fast. Women deserve better. They deserve compensation for their role in sustaining our society, and ensuring that they are fairly compensated menas breaking down barriers of social stigma, but it also means breaking down the same barriers that happen to catch the rest of labor in the crossfire.
