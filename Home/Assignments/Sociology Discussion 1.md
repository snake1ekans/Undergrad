˚I think the most impactful discussion for me was the one of **education**. I grew up in a place with very few non-white students, but having an understanding of how my high school's dynamics and student outcomes were shaped by the same "race-blind" policies that led to segregation has definitely been a good opportunity for self-reflection. 

My high school was a Title 1 school but also had a lot of upper-middle class students because it covered a lot of geography. Because of that, the **social stratification** between different elementary schools which all covered different neighborhoods of different financial standings was pretty severe. The "smart kids" tended to come from the nicer neighborhoods, and this meant that pretty quickly there was a lot of separation between who took which class. This hasn't borne out nearly as strictly in who is where in life now, but I know that there's a big difference in who went on to pursue higher education, who went on to pursue the trades, and who went straight into the workforce. 

The other big effect that I've been able to understand a little better as a result of this class is the effect that different funding levels and schools have on the **Agents of Socialization**. I went to a specialty school part-time while I was in high school, and that meant that I got to see how different educational models, funding levels, and financial situations played into the agents of socialization in schools specifically. The specialty school was a mixed-learning college-integrated campus, meaning that most of the teachers were able to be 100-level professors who didn't have to lecture but more often served as tutors while the Canvas courses did most of the teaching. My peers were also more academically-inclined, as the school required a certain level of academic performance to attend. All of the agents at the school, from peers to teachers and even the principal (who would walk around to make sure we were fairly on-task), were directed towards socializing students toward higher education, academia, and specifically engineering. 

This contrasts rather sharply with the high school I attended the other part of the time. While I spent most of my time in that school focused on things not offered at the magnet (such as band and debate), it gave me a look at what different funding and focus can do to change the dynamic of a school. While the specialty school was very focused on education and had a rightful reputation of being a little weird, the social dynamics at the regular school were more stratified (because there wasn't an inherent selection like with the specialty school) but also there was something that I can now put my finger on that was different. The arts and extracurricular, major agents of socialization were different than my specialty-school peers, a lot of whom did the same program I did. The biggest difference was funding. The funding of the arts at the other schools was usually better, to the point that they would have more choirs, more expensive theater productions, and better band programs. The example that comes to mind is that despite a rival high school being much smaller, they had a full marching band, whereas our high school struggled to fill a 16-man drumline every year. This meant that less kids were socialized to appreciate the arts, and it meant that the school's sources of school unity weren't as strong. That's borne out in how my high school class has kind of fractured after graduation, with pretty strong stratification between groups, academic levels, and neighborhoods.
