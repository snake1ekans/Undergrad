Enos
Trying translation

This is a very personal and special passage to me. Enos was the spiritual lightning rod that provided the confirmation needed for me to continue with my mission. In the spirit of my mission, reading the scriptures in alternate (and especially original) languages can provide insights into meaning that the translators might have taken out that I hadn't. Thus, thought-for-thought translation of enos would look something like:

(V. 3 to 12, versification removed.)
Behold, I left to hunt beasts in the forest, and the words that I had often heard from my father touching on eternal life and the joy of the saints deeply penetrated my heart and my soul hungered. I knelt before my Maker and cried to him in potent prayer for my soul. I cried to him then entire day, and as night fell I still raised my voice that it reached the heavens.
A voice came, saying: "Enos, your sins are pardoned, and you will be blessed." I, Enos, knew that God could not lie and my guilt was erased. I said, "Lord, how is this brought about?" and he said to me, "by your faith in Christ, who you have never and will never hear nor see, for many years will pass before before he becomes flesh, thus your faith has healed you." 
When I heard these words, I began to spill my soul out to God because of my anxiety for the wellbeing of my kinsmen the nephites. As I struggled in the spirit, the voice of the Lord again pierced my mind, saying: I will visit your kinsmen according to their diligence in keeping my commandments. I have given them this holy land, and I do not curse it except due to iniquity. Therefore, I will visit them as I have said, and I will make their failings fall painfully upon their own heads. When I had heard this, my faith became unbreakable and I prayed mightily for my brethren the lamanites. After I had prayed and exerted myself completely, the Lord said unto me, "because of your faith, I will give unto you according to your desires."

This translation is adapted from a loose translation of the Spanish text when compared with the English text and then modernized. It provides a lot of insights that may be obscured by modern interpretive choices or word duality provided by English. 

In verse 6, the language is much stronger in Spanish and Haitian Kreyol. "Expurgada"(expunged) or "efase" (erased). While this is a legal term and I am personally against a legal framework for understanding sin, forgiveness, and the atonement, there is validity in understanding that our sins are expurgated, or completely removed from our own personal record of our actions. The argument for a legalistic model of the atonement loses support when verse 8 is considered. This verse is much more clear about the healing nature of atonement and reflects the more ancient Judaic understanding of sin and penalty. According to Terryl Givens, the earliest concepts of sin in Judaism were not of payment and penalty, but of hurt and healing. I would argue that that concept is no more clearly found in the Book of Mormon than here. 

The process of paraphrasing passages is one of my favorite ways to understand scripture because oftentimes synonyms come with several different subtexts and secondary uses (While PC, Desktop, and Computer are synonyms, they have very different uses). This also results in a consideration of both authorial and translational intent,

Paraphrasing can also lead to a better understanding of the authorial intent because it leads (usually) to a better understanding of the context surrounding a passage. For example, the context surrounding the word pistis in Greek (which is commonly translated as faith) has a context that is well outside the protestant faith - grace debate that martin luther started and john calvin and his followers completely butchered.
