The passage that I chose today is not one that I had considered, but one that definitely has a lot of value as a passage for teaching. As such, I chose to practice refining my devotional style and gave a lukewarm defense of Laman and Lemuel (though ultimately falling on the side of Nephi and Lehi). There are two important passages to consider and a few critical pieces of context that help us paint a fuller picture of Laman and Lemuel's motivations as more than just one-dimensional absolute opposition to the absolute goodness of Nephi  and better understand the complex family dynamic at play.

To start, some critical context. Up to the point of the Book of Mormon in Jewish history, there was one long-standing promise: as long as the Israelites were obedient and faithful, they would always hold the land of promise. When they weren't, they were exiled. The other important piece of context is that the Book of Mormon happens during the Reign of Zedekiah, who was a puppet king put in by Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon. The most critical part of the context is that Nebuchadnezzar had already taken away the sacred relics of the Temple outlined in the Torah at this point. The temple was defiled, and for most, the writing was on the wall for Jerusalem. 

According to all this, we have the story of a family. Lehi, the patriarch, receives a vision that the Jews are, in fact, rather wicked and thus unworthy to possess the holy land. He is rejected by the people, and later by his own sons. Laman, Lemuel, and Nephi were all to varying degrees unsure or actively against their father's direction to move out of Jerusalem, and they had valid spiritual reason to believe so. As 1 Nephi 2:13 states, they "did not believe that Jerusalem could be destroyed according to the words of the prophets. While that phrasing is rather ambiguous, it can be interpreted as the destruction prophesied by the likes of Jeremiah and Lehi, but it could also easily be the words of earlier prophets, those that promised Israel that continued faith would assure the sanctity of Jerusalem. 

That sanctity is one that Laman and Lemuel may well have believed existed. Their own household was led by a devout patriarch, and according to 1 Nephi 17:22, they claimed that they believed the inhabitants of Jerusalem to be a "righteous people," one who "kept the statues and judgements of the Lord." While this claim may seem difficult in the face of overt abomination, the likelihood is that there weren't happenings in the street every 5 seconds, but rather as Nephi later states, that the people were hardening and had hardened their hearts continually before the Lord.

This brings us to the second passage of scripture cited: 1 Nephi 2:12, which states that their hearts were hard because "they knew not the dealings of that God who had created them." While Laman and Lemuel may have had good reason to rely on the word of ancient prophesy regarding their security and their opinion that the Jews under Zedekiah were a just people, they ignored the obvious: the lack of a sacred space of worship, and a man being called of God to tell them to get out of Dodge.

However, our fine protagonist (and narrator) may well have had the same feelings. He alludes to the fact that his heart was softened after praying to the Lord with real intent. The difference between Nephi and his brothers, however, rests in the fact that he understood the dealings of the Lord, and that he will always continually reveal his will unto the faithful. As LDS academic Ben Spackman once said, a prophet's words generally don't get better with age. Taken to my generation, a prophet's words age like milk: They are usually best enjoyed fresh and with good contemplation, and occasionally they will develop into a fine, complex cheese. But most of the time, they just spoil. They get chunky, rough, and sour in a world that constantly needs different guidance to match their circumstances. Laman and Lemuel's problem was not that they were enjoying the cheese, it was that they were ignoring the milk of the prophet's words.
