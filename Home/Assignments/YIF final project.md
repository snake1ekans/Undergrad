While the primary article briefly touches on the mechanisms and underpinning evolutionary context, there is lots to be gleaned from diving deeper on the studies cited, especially discussing the mechanisms by which these animals resist cancer's development and growth.

The first relevant example is that of the naked mole rat, whose life-span is 10-15 times longer than that of an equivalent-sized mouse. The study cited, "The Naked Mole Rat Genome Resource: facilitating analyses of cancer and longevity-related adaptations" re-sequenced the genome of the naked mole rat, and in the process identified increased frequencies of certain important alleles, particularly TP53, which we will discuss next, and posits an example of convergent evolution between that and humans, who also have one copy of this particular gene.

The second example of the article was the majestic elephant, whose resistance to cancer is correlated to duplicate versions of a specific gene. According to "Potential Mechanisms for Cancer Resistance in Elephants and Comparative Cellular Response to DNA Damage in Humans" by Abegglen et al. 2015) This study identifies duplicate copies of the TP53 allele, which is a sequence of DNA that codes for the protein p53, which is an essential protein for causing apoptosis and preventing the proliferation and metastasis of cancerous cells. While human cells only contain 2 copies of the allele, elephant cells contain many more copies of the allele. 

In the second study, they intentionally exposed cells with many vs few copies of TP53 to large amounts of radiation and examined the cells afterwards. They found that the cells with more TP53 were more resistant to cancer. While the study itself calls for more testing on the subject, it does clearly correlate the higher presence of TP53 to higher cancer resistance.
